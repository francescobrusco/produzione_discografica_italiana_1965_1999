The social network of the Italian record industry

The twenty best-selling albums of each year, from 1965 to 1999, and their paratexts are the primary sources for reconstructing the social network of studio production. Every sleeve credit becomes a connection.

The network is multimodal: it includes individuals (performers, musicians, producers, arrangers, engineers), collective subjects (bands, orchestras, choirs), institutional nodes (record companies), albums as projects that catalyse relationships, and recording studios, each of which acts as a focus: an aggregator of interactions between human and material agents.

Processed in Gephi, the matrices drawn from the corpus produce a graph of 3410 nodes and 7926 edges: dense in appearance, but with very low density, a sign of a high degree of homophily. Nodes condense by geographical, stylistic and professional affinity into relatively tight local clusters.

Who makes up the network

The breakdown by category reveals some peculiarities. Sound engineers are by far the largest category, almost twice as many as studios and producers, while mastering engineers close the list. The 93 record companies (2.69%) point to the oligopolistic nature of the industry; among session players, backing vocalists outnumber guitarists and keyboard players.

Communities and boundaries

The modularity algorithm identifies five main subgroups, each centred on a record company and a reference studio. They are not watertight compartments: they permeate each other through the people who work at their intersections. The sharpest boundary separates the fields of RCA and CGD, the two leading labels of the period.

There were all these circles, so actual rhythm sections had formed, each had its own rhythm section […] this one is a Numero Uno sound, this is Ricordi, this is an RCA sound.Ellade Bandini, interview, 23 May 2023 (translated from Italian)

Development over time

Divided into five-year periods, the network expands from the 1970s and condenses in the following decade. 1980–1984 is the moment of greatest cooperation: highest average degree (3.83), lowest diameter (8), modularity and path length. 1990–1994 is the only period in which the network forms a single component, on the eve of the definitive rise of digital technology.

Labels and studios, studios and engineers

In the label–studio dyad most nodes maintain non-exclusive ties: each studio works with several labels and vice versa. The four major record companies are the exception. RCA replicates the model of its American parent company, with a large in-house studio that draws the other Roman studios into its orbit; CGD absorbs small private studios and borders on Clan Celentano; Ricordi overlaps with CBS near Lucio Battisti’s Mulino; EMI bridges the Mersey and the Po.

The studio–sound engineer–mastering triad makes visible the professional divide between in-house engineers, whose node often touches or overlaps that of the studio, and freelancers. Mastering moves beyond the idea of a mere technical transfer only at the end of the 1970s, following the Anglo-American model.

At that precise moment I decided to go freelance, to find new stimuli, new things, new equipment to use.Piero Bravin, interview, 25 September 2023 (translated from Italian)

Three ego-nets

Mina, Paolo Gianolio and Renato Cantele: three professional roles, three forms of personal network. The guitarist sits at the centre of a homogeneous ego-net; Mina’s, the highest-degree performer, closes into a tight circle of trust of only 200 nodes. The ego-net visualisations were also the starting point for most of the interviews: the interviewees “recognised” themselves in them.

She either loves you or shuts you out, but in a… polite way.Massimo Moriconi, interview, 23 October 2023 (translated from Italian)

A man’s story?

With women making up 7.8% of the network, the Italian record industry of the period shows a marked gender imbalance, with no sign of change. Women professionals are concentrated in singing, the “front-facing” role par excellence, and their presence collapses in technical and production jobs: 2.3% of producers, none in mastering.

It was obvious that my opinion counted for less than my colleagues’. Despite my experience.Marti Jane Robertson, interview, 20 November 2023 (translated from Italian)

Recording studios in Italy

The recording studio is the focus of the network: a physical space that brings together people, technologies, conventions and tastes. Their number grows throughout the period, with the highest rates in the first three five-year periods.

In 1965–1969 only 31% of records name the studio, and there are nine of them: RCA in Rome, the rest in Milan. In the 1970s the system remains bipolar, but small hubs open in Genoa and Venice, along with the first residential studios outside the cities, such as Stone Castle in Carimate and Battisti’s Mulino. In 1980–1984 Carimate takes the lead from RCA for the first time, while a third hub, Bologna and Modena, emerges along the Via Emilia. The 1990s bring fragmentation and the handover from analogue to digital.

        Milan, Rome, Bologna

        The three city networks are not separate: many nodes appear in all three music worlds. Milan and Bologna share more connections, while Rome remains off-centre.

        Milan

        Italy’s music city par excellence sees recording rooms flourish in every corner: Fonorama and Ricordi to the south, EMI’s Basilica in Piazza Sant’Eufemia, then the Via Quintiliano hub near Linate airport (CGD, Idea Recording, Morning, Logic) and the residential studios in Brianza.

        Rome

        The capital is dominated by RCA’s large complex, opened at the end of 1961 at km 12 of the Via Tiburtina. Around it, studios spring up drawn by its business and by that of RAI and Cinecittà: Ortophonic, Sonic, Studio 38, Quattro Uno, Forum Music Village.

        Bologna

        From the late 1970s Emilia develops its own network, thanks to falling technology costs, a favourable property market and the milieu of the DAMS, free radio and the 1977 movement. Fonoprint is its centre, with Modena and Reggio Emilia along the Via Emilia.

        Method and sources

        How it was built

        The corpus gathers the twenty best-selling Italian albums of each year between 1965 and 1999, drawn from FIMI, Musica e dischi and Hit Parade Italia. For each album the credits on sleeves and booklets were transcribed and supplemented with archival sources and interviews with the people involved.

        Each credit becomes an undirected edge between a person (or a studio, or a label) and an album. The network was analysed with social network analysis in Gephi and with geo-historical mapping. The dataset is based on official charts: informal circulation is deliberately left out of scope.
